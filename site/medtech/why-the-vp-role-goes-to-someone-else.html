← Coaching for sales directors in medical devices

Why does the VP role keep going to someone else when your numbers are better?

“I hit the number three years running. They gave the VP job to someone who missed theirs.”

It is one of the most common frustrations I hear from sales directors in medical devices. You do what the business asks. Your region delivers. Then the vice president role opens, and it goes to someone whose numbers were weaker than yours.

It feels unfair. Usually it is not about fairness. It is about what the people deciding are actually looking for.

Results get you to director. They do not get you to VP.

At director level, the number is the job. At vice president level, the number is assumed. The people choosing are asking a different question: can this person run a part of the business, not just deliver it?

They are looking for someone who already thinks about where the portfolio is going, how NHS and hospital customers are changing, what global is likely to ask for next year, and who can hold a room of peers without the forecast in front of them.

If every conversation they have had with you is about this quarter, they have no evidence that you think any further ahead. Not because you don’t. Because they never see it.

Three things usually get in the way

  • Your time goes on the number. Forecast calls, escalations, the deals that need you. The bigger thinking happens, if at all, in the car.
  • Your view stays in your head. You have opinions on where the business should go. You rarely say them first, in front of the people who decide.
  • Nobody tells you how you come across. Your team won’t. Your boss is busy. So you keep doing what got you here.

The person who got the job may simply have been more visible doing vice president thinking. That is something you can learn.

What to try this week

  1. Pick one question about the business beyond your own region: a product line, a shift in how customers buy, a change in how the NHS is commissioning. Give it one hour, on paper.
  2. Take your view to your VP before they ask for it. Not a forecast update. "Here is what I think we should do about this, and why."
  3. Ask one person you trust: "When you think of me, what do you think I am good at?" Listen for whether they mention anything beyond results.

None of this means caring less about the number. It means letting the people who decide see the rest of what you can do.

Where coaching helps

This is most of what I work on with sales directors: finding one protected hour a week for the bigger question, getting your own view clear enough to say out loud, and an honest read on how you come across in the rooms that matter.
