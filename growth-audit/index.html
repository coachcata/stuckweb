The Growth Audit

Why the business stopped growing

Three weeks, one thousand nine hundred and ninety five pounds, and a written answer you can act on. For owners of firms between one and five million who are working as hard as they ever have and cannot see why the numbers have flattened.

The question it answers

You already know the symptoms.

Revenue in roughly the same band for three years. A team that is busy. Costs that have moved and prices that have not. And a growing suspicion that the answer is somewhere you cannot see from inside it.

Your accountant can show you what happened. What the numbers cannot tell you is why, because the cause is almost never in them.

What it examines

The whole business, through one lens.

I look at the same things any decent diagnostic would. Where the money comes from and what it costs to get. How the work actually flows. Which clients are worth having. What the team can and cannot do. Where time goes.

The lens is different. At this size, the constraint is usually not strategy and it is rarely effort. It is that a specific set of decisions can only be made by one person, and that person is you. So every finding gets tested against a single question: what would have to be true for this to happen without you.

What you get

In writing. Ranked. With the evidence attached.

  1. An outside read of the business, from somebody with no stake in the answer
  2. A method rather than an opinion, so you can see how each conclusion was reached
  3. Three structural risks, ranked in order of what they are costing you. Always three
  4. All of it in writing, so you can sit with it rather than remember it
  5. The evidence behind each finding, so you can check it rather than take my word
  6. A clear split between what you can act on alone and what would need somebody alongside you
  7. A fixed price and a defined end. Three weeks, and then it is finished

That last one matters more than it sounds. Open ended consulting is how most owners at this size have been burnt before, and I would rather you knew the shape of this before you agreed to it.

The deliverables

Three things, and the report is the spine.

OneThe report

Eight to twelve pages of prose. Not slides. Written to be read twice, and to survive being forwarded to the one person you trust.

TwoThe working session

Ninety minutes, you and me, working through what the report found. It is not always comfortable. It is the part owners tell me they remember.

ThreeThe debriefs

Every leader who took a CliftonStrengths assessment gets their own debrief. Your team gets something directly out of this, rather than only being reported on.

What is in the report

  1. A one-page summary at the front, written so it can be forwarded on its own
  2. The question you asked, in your words, on page one
  3. What we found
  4. Three ranked structural risks. Always three, never a list that trails off
  5. Sequenced ninety-day recommendations, at least half of them actionable without me
How it runs

Three weeks

Week one

A single kickoff of ninety minutes, where you state the question this audit answers in your own words. That sentence goes on page one of the report, and everything after it is judged against it.

Week two

Fieldwork. A confidential interview and a CliftonStrengths assessment for each leader in scope, and structural analysis of how decisions actually flow and what depends on whom. Not a staff survey. I am checking whether what you have told me matches what happens on a Tuesday.

Week three

The report is written, and we spend ninety minutes working through it together. The plan is in your hands within twenty-four hours of that session.

Where it stops

The edges, stated before you buy.

One confidential interview and one CliftonStrengths assessment for each leader in scope. Up to six of them on the standard engagement, seven to nine on the larger one. Above nine it stops being a fixed product, so we talk about the shape of it before we start rather than after.

The report is delivered once. It is a diagnosis, not a document we redraft together until everybody is comfortable with it.

Implementation, workshops and ongoing coaching are separate engagements, priced separately, if and when you want them. Keeping them out is what keeps three weeks to three weeks.

Anything touching tax, funding structures or formal valuation goes back to the referring accountant, by name.

Those are their questions, not mine, and the report says so in writing rather than quietly straying into them. If you came here through your accountant, they stay in the loop throughout and nothing in this cuts across them.

What it usually finds

The constraint is a person, and it is usually you.

Not because of anything you have done wrong. Because you built the thing, and the judgement you make forty times a week has never been built in anybody else, and that cannot be documented or recruited.

When that is the finding, the audit produces the plan for fixing it: what somebody else would need to be able to do, what could be handed over and in what order, and what would need to change about how you hand things over. Whether you do that with me afterwards is a separate conversation, and the report is yours either way.

When it is not that

Sometimes the answer is documentation.

If nothing has ever been written down and you are answering the same question forty times a week, that is a documentation problem and it has a straightforward fix. If the constraint is capacity rather than judgement, you need to hire rather than develop. If it is pricing, it is arithmetic and a difficult fortnight.

All of those are real findings and I have delivered all of them. In each case you leave with something you can act on without me, which is the point.
