An Hour at the Board, On Your Own.

A quiet guide to running your own thinking hour. No sign-up, no catch. Take it and use it.

Print this page

There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from carrying a question for too long. Not the tiredness of hard work, which at least resolves into sleep, but the low hum of something unresolved that travels with you into meetings, into the drive home, into the minutes before you fall asleep. If you are reading this, you probably know the question I mean. You may have been carrying it for weeks.

This piece is a way of putting it down. Not forever, and not by ignoring it, but by giving it an hour of the right conditions and seeing what it becomes. I run sessions like this with leaders at a whiteboard, and the method is simple enough that you can run a version of it alone. So here it is, given away properly, with nothing held back.

You will need an hour, a door that closes, and a surface. A whiteboard if you have one, but a large sheet of paper works, or a glass wall, or a flipchart pad from the stationery cupboard. What matters is that it is big enough that you have to stand up and move to use it. The standing matters more than it sounds. Thinking that happens in a chair, in front of a screen, tends to loop. Thinking that happens on your feet, in front of a surface, tends to travel.

Choosing the question

Most leaders, given an hour to think, spend the first twenty minutes thinking about the wrong thing. So before the hour starts, ask yourself one question: what is actually live? Not what is most important on paper, and not what you feel you ought to think about, but the thing that keeps surfacing on its own. The decision you keep circling. The situation you keep turning over in the shower. Your mind has already voted. Trust the vote.

Write that question at the centre of the board, in as few words as you can manage. If it takes a full sentence, fine, but try for less. "Restructure, yes or no." "What do I do about Sarah." "Why am I hesitating on the acquisition." The act of compressing it is the first piece of real work, because it forces you to decide what the question actually is, and you may find it is not quite the one you thought you were carrying.

Getting it out of your head

Now empty everything. For the next fifteen or twenty minutes, your only job is to get what is in your head onto the surface. Every consideration, every worry, every half-thought, every "yes but", every name, every number you are holding. Branch them off the central question in whatever arrangement occurs to you. Do not organise. Do not evaluate. Do not stop to wonder whether something deserves to be up there. If it is in your head, it goes on the board.

This will feel untidy, and it should. The mess is the point. What you are doing is taking a set of thoughts that have been circling each other in the dark, colliding and repeating, and laying them out where they can all be seen at once. A mind like ours holds an enormous amount, and holding is expensive. Every thought you park on the board is a thought you no longer have to carry, and you can feel the weight shift as it happens.

You will know this stage is done when you slow down. When you stand back and find yourself reaching for things to add rather than rushing to capture them, the head is empty enough. Stand back properly now, a few steps, and look at the whole board.

Looking at what is actually there

This is the moment the surface earns its keep. Almost always, a board holds fewer things than the mind believed it was carrying. What felt like thirty tangled considerations turns out to be nine, and three of them are the same consideration wearing different clothes. Circle the duplicates. Draw a line between the things that are connected. Notice which branch has the most weight on it, because that is usually where the real question lives, and it is not always the one you wrote in the centre.

Then ask yourself the quiet question that changes most of these hours: what am I looking at? Not "what should I do", not yet. Just, what is this? Is it a decision waiting for courage rather than information? Is it two problems knotted together that need separating? Is it a question about the business that is actually a question about a person? Say the answer out loud if you can. There is something about hearing your own voice name the thing that no amount of silent thought quite achieves.

When it stalls

Somewhere past the half-hour, the thinking may stall. You have everything out, you can see it all, and yet the way forward is not appearing. This is normal, and it is worth knowing what it usually means, because a stall is nearly always one of three things.

Sometimes it is a missing piece of information, in which case the hour has done its job by finding the gap. Write the question you cannot answer in a corner of the board, note who or what could answer it, and let the hour end there with a next move rather than a conclusion. That is a good outcome, not a failed one.

Sometimes it is an option you are refusing to look at. If the thinking keeps sliding away from one particular branch, walk toward it deliberately. Ask yourself what would have to be true for that path to be the right one. You do not have to choose it. You just have to stop steering around it, because an avoided option distorts every other option on the board.

And sometimes the stall is the honest signal that this particular question does not resolve alone, because what it needs is not more thought but another voice, someone to ask the question you cannot ask yourself. If that is where you land, that is also a real finding. Note where you got to, because the thinking you have done is not wasted, it is the raw material for whatever conversation comes next.

Knowing when you have landed

You will know the difference between a thought and a landing because a landing is quiet. The circling stops. The question, when you look at it, no longer pulls at you. Often what you land on is smaller and plainer than you expected, a single next move, a conversation you now know you have to have, a decision that was made weeks ago and was only waiting to be admitted. Do not distrust it for being simple. The simplicity is what all the carrying was in the way of.

Before you leave the room, write the landing down in one sentence, somewhere other than the board. Photograph the board too, but the sentence is the thing. A decision that is not written down has a way of quietly unmaking itself by Thursday, and the sentence is your anchor against the second-guessing that visits every considered mind after it commits.

Then rub the board clean. That part is more important than it sounds.

That is the whole method. Nothing is held back, and there is no next step hidden behind it. Run it whenever a question has been travelling with you for too long, and it will serve you.

I will be honest about the one thing the hour alone cannot give you, because you would notice its absence anyway. When I sit with a leader at the board, the thing I add is not the method, which you now have, but the other voice. The question you would not think to ask yourself, the branch you steer around without noticing, the moment someone says "you have written the answer three times in different words." Some questions land alone. Some only land out loud, in company. You will know which kind you are carrying, and either way, the board will tell you more than the chair ever will.
